The Hierarchy of Skills: Stranger to Guide

ACEM tracks evolution using Bloom's Taxonomy. The assessment must be **Objective, Consistent, and Contextual**.

The ACEM Structure

Bloom's Taxonomy Integration

🎯 **Assessor Guide: The 3 C's of Assessment**

A score is valid only if it meets these criteria for the assessor (Teacher/Manager):

  • **Consistency:** The same behavior must yield the same score across all staff and assessors.
  • **Contextuality:** The skill is only relevant when applied in the operational context (Situation $\ne$ Theory).
  • **Clarity/Objectivity:** Use only observable metrics. Never score on "attitude" or "feeling."

Rule: If you can't measure it, don't score it.

📊 **ACEM Component Breakdown**

The total ACEM Score is the average of these four core components:

  • **A - Accountability:** Reliability, Time Management, Taking Ownership.
  • **C - Competency:** Technical Skill mastery and efficiency (measured against SOPs).
  • **E - Engagement:** Proactive communication, team support, seeking growth (self-study).
  • **M - Mentorship/Mastery:** Ability to teach others and innovate processes (Level 3+).

Diagnosing Leadership & The 5 Vices

The GLSM determines how we manage staff based on their ACEM score, using Hersey-Blanchard's Model.

➡️ **GLSM Management Flowchart (Operational Process)**
  1. **Assess ACEM:** Determine the staff member's current ACEM score (D/R level).
  2. **Diagnose Readiness:** Match D-level to the appropriate S-level (S1, S2, S3, or S4).
  3. **Apply Strategy:** Implement the corresponding management behavior (Directing, Coaching, Supporting, or Delegating).
  4. **Monitor & Adjust:** Regularly reassess ACEM score; if performance changes, move to the next strategy (Flow is circular).

This process ensures managerial effort is **aligned with staff need**.

🎓 **Situational Leadership Styles (S1, S2, S3, S4)**

These four styles define the management strategy based on the employee's **Readiness Level (D)**, clearly diagnosing if the issue is **Skill** (Competence) or **Will** (Commitment).

StyleReadiness (D)Skill/Will DiagnosisManager Action
**S1: Directing** D1: Low Competence, High Commitment (New Starter) **Skill Issue:** Needs Training/Guidance. Tell them *what* to do, *how* to do it, and *when* to do it (High Task).
**S2: Coaching** D2: Low-to-Some Competence, Low Commitment (Disillusioned Learner) **Skill & Will Issue:** Needs Training AND Motivation/Clarification. Explain decisions, clarify tasks, and provide encouragement (High Task, High Relation).
**S3: Supporting** D3: Moderate-to-High Competence, Variable Commitment (Capable but Cautious) **Will Issue:** Needs Motivation/Confidence. Facilitate, listen, and encourage self-direction. Share responsibility for decision-making (High Relation, Low Task).
**S4: Delegating** D4: High Competence, High Commitment (Master/Expert) **No Issue:** Self-reliant and Motivated. Turn over responsibility for decision-making and problem-solving to them (Low Task, Low Relation).

Historical Models of Leadership Traits

Defining the objective traits required for success, moving from single theories to combined influences.

1. Ralph Stogdill's Findings (1948 & 1974)

Stogdill noted that a successful leader must possess traits **relevant to the situation**, arguing against a universal trait list. His analysis yielded two key lists of distinguishing traits:

1948 Findings (Eight Traits):

  • Intelligence, Alertness, Insight.
  • Responsibility, Initiative, Persistence.
  • Self-confidence, Sociability.

1974 Findings (Trait and Situational Factors):

  • Achievement, Persistence, Insight, Initiative, Self-confidence, Responsibility.
  • Cooperativeness, Tolerance, Influence, Sociability.

The key shift was acknowledging the importance of **both** trait and situational factors.

2. McCall and Lombardo (1983)

Examined both success and failure, identifying four critical factors:

  • Emotional Stability.
  • Admitting Mistakes.
  • Good Interpersonal Skills.
  • Intellectual Breadth.
3. Kouzes and Posner (1987) - The Follower Perspective

Derived from surveying followers on the qualities they valued in great leaders:

  • Honest, Forward-looking, Inspirational, Competent.
  • Fair-minded, Supportive, Broad-minded, Intelligent.
  • Straightforward, Dependable.
4. Kirkpatrick & Locke (1991) - The Basic Toolset

Suggested these traits equip leaders with the tools to develop into great leaders:

  • Drive, Leadership Motivation.
  • Honesty and Integrity.
  • Self-confidence.
  • Cognitive Ability & Knowledge of the Business.
5. Zaccaro, Kemp, and Bader (2004) - Trait Interaction

Proposed that leadership emerges from the **combined influence and interaction** between multiple traits, not traits existing independently:

  • Extraversion, Cognitive abilities, Conscientiousness, Emotional stability, Openness, Agreeableness, Motivation.
  • Social intelligence, Self-monitoring, Emotional intelligence, Problem-solving.

Understanding the "Nafs" (Self)

We combine philosophical (Aristotle) and empirical (Seligman, Big Five) models to understand staff personality profiles.

1. Seligman's VIA Classification vs. Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics

The distinction lies in the approach to excellence ($eudaimonia$):

  • VIA Classification: Modern, **empirical, descriptive** framework (24 strengths). Goal is flourishing by utilizing individual **"signature strengths."**
  • Aristotle's Ethics: **Prescriptive philosophical** work. Goal is the highest good by cultivating virtue through finding the **"golden mean"** using **$phronesis$ (practical wisdom).**
2. VIA Classification of Character Strengths (24 Strengths, 6 Virtues)

The 24 specific character strengths are grouped under six broad moral virtues:

1. Wisdom and Knowledge: Creativity, Curiosity, Judgment/Critical Thinking, Love of Learning, Perspective/Wisdom.

2. Courage: Bravery, Honesty/Integrity, Perseverance/Persistence, Zest/Vitality.

3. Humanity: Kindness/Generosity, Love, Social Intelligence.

4. Justice: Fairness, Leadership, Teamwork/Citizenship.

5. Temperance: Forgiveness/Mercy, Humility/Modesty, Prudence, Self-Regulation/Self-Control.

6. Transcendence: Appreciation of Beauty & Excellence, Gratitude, Hope/Optimism, Humor/Playfulness, Spirituality/Purpose.

3. NEO-PI-R 30 Facets (The Big Five Breakdown)

Measures five broad personality domains (The "Big Five") through 30 specific facets:

  • Neuroticism (N): Anxiety, Angry Hostility, Depression, Self-Consciousness, Impulsiveness, Vulnerability.
  • Extraversion (E): Warmth, Gregariousness, Assertiveness, Activity, Excitement Seeking, Positive Emotions.
  • Openness to Experience (O): Fantasy, Aesthetics, Feelings, Actions, Ideas, Values.
  • Agreeableness (A): Trust, Straightforwardness, Altruism, Compliance, Modesty, Tender-Mindedness.
  • Conscientiousness (C): Competence, Order, Dutifulness, Achievement Striving, Self-Discipline, Deliberation.
4. Colin DeYoung & Jordan Peterson 10 Aspects (Sub-Factors)

Identified two distinct "aspects" (sub-factors) within each of the five Big Five domains:

  • Openness: Intellect (abstract ideas) & Openness (aesthetics/imagination).
  • Conscientiousness: Industriousness (work ethic) & Orderliness (tidiness).
  • Extraversion: Assertiveness (dominance) & Enthusiasm (sociability).
  • Agreeableness (A): Compassion (empathy) & Politeness (social norms).
  • Neuroticism (N): Withdrawal (anxiety/sadness) & Volatility (irritability/mood swings).

🧭 **Strategic Alignment: The Value Pyramid**

The Value Pyramid provides a hierarchy for motivating and aligning staff (Will/Commitment). As staff progress through ACEM levels, their motivation should move up the pyramid:

  • **ACEM Level 1/2 (Survival/Security):** Motivated by **Happiness, Cool, Popularity, Prestige** (Base of the Pyramid).
  • **ACEM Level 3 (Growth/Mastery):** Motivated by **Money** and **Excellence**.
  • **ACEM Level 4 (Guide/Entrepreneur):** Motivated by **Impact** and **Truth** (Apex of the Pyramid).
